# Compound nouns, and the words they are made of.
#
# One line per compound: the word, a tab, then its parts. A sentence saying a
# compound listed ABOVE THE MARKER counts as saying its parts instead, so a
# reader who knows `krank` and `haus` can read `Krankenhaus` without it being
# taught as a word of its own.
#
# ONLY LINES ABOVE THE MARKER COUNT. Below it these are guesses, made by
# cutting wherever both halves are words the corpus says at least thirty
# times. Check each one; delete what is wrong; move the marker down. Nothing
# unverified ever reaches the corpus.
#
# Nouns only, decided by capitalisation -- German writes its nouns with a
# capital, so a lemma whose surfaces are 80% capitalised is one. That test
# removes the family that made the earlier version unusable: separable verb
# prefixes split cleanly and mean something else entirely (ueberleben is not
# ueber + leben, uebernehmen is not ueber + nehmen), and adverbs like
# vielleicht and teilweise merely look compound.
#
# Two filters came before it, each fixing the version above:
#   parts must be words the corpus says 30+ times, or `politisch` comes out
#   as `polit` + `isch` and a hundred rejections sit at the top;
#   parts must not be suffixes, or every `-lich` and `-ung` word decomposes.
#
# Ordered by how often the corpus says each word, most frequent first, so the
# checking pays earliest. The number after each line is that count.

# grounds that a reader who knows the parts can get the word. Seven were
# deleted rather than kept, and they are worth knowing as a shape:
#
#     hochzeit     = hoch + zeit     "wedding", not "high time"
#     fruehstueck  = frueh + stueck  "breakfast"
#     unterlage    = unter + lage    "documents"
#     nachteil     = nach + teil     "disadvantage"
#     lebensmittel = leben + mittel  "groceries"
#     gegensatz    = gegen + satz    Satz is "setting" here, not "sentence"
#     wasserstoff  = wasser + stoff  "hydrogen" -- chemistry, not German
#
# `hochzeit` is the one to remember: it splits perfectly, both parts are
# common words, and the answer is completely wrong. Nothing automatic
# catches that -- only knowing what the word means.
#
# Lines marked "the meaning is a stretch" are kept but unsettled: they
# decompose correctly and land near the sense without giving it, like the
# -zeug family. Whether they belong is a question about teaching rather
# than about German.

# German only inserts that s at a real seam -- Arbeit+s+Platz -- so it
# is good evidence the split is genuine, and those are the cheapest to
# judge. Not proof, though: `schriftsteller` is schrift + steller, and
# the s belongs to the second half rather than the join.
# After them, the rest by frequency as before.#
